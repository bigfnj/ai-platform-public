# Who actually decides in a 20-person business, and how do they buy?

> Source: https://blogs.microsoft.com/wp-content/uploads/prod/2022/09/Microsoft-Small-and-Medium-Sized-Business-Voice-and-Attitudes-to-Technology-Study-2022.pdf (Microsoft / Analysys Mason, July 2022)
> As of: 2022-07 · Retrieved: 2026-08-14

## Who is the technology decision-maker in an SMB?

Rarely a full-time IT person, and almost never only an IT person. Microsoft's commissioned SMB study found that technology decision-makers in SMBs "tend to be responsible for more than just IT," with **41% also responsible for product development**, **33% for operations**, **30% for procurement**, **28% for customer service** and **26% for marketing**.

That single finding should reshape how a partner sells. The person you are talking to is not evaluating your proposal against other technology proposals; they are evaluating it against every other call on their attention that week, most of which are operational. The study's own language is that SMBs "may be more heavily influenced by individual executives responsible for decision-making, resulting in businesses that act more akin to consumers."

Demographically, the study found decision-makers were 79% male and 21% female, and generationally split with roughly 50% Gen X, 41% Millennial, 7% Boomer and about 1% Gen Z.

## Does a 20-person business have IT staff at all?

More often than partners assume, but not the kind of IT staff that can evaluate a platform decision. The study found **77% of SMBs have either full-time or part-time internal IT employees** (64% full-time dedicated, 27% part-time), yet also that **45% rely on external business consultants or partners**, and 26% have non-IT internal employees managing technology.

The critical qualifier is what those internal staff actually do. Microsoft's research describes SMBs as tending "to hire generalist IT personnel who can handle day-to-day issues such as systems management and maintenance," and states that internal IT staff "rarely specialize in areas that are essential to the adoption of new technology, such as platform development or cyber security." Very small businesses are much less likely — 45% — to have dedicated full-time internal IT staff at all, because they "lack the size and flow of continuous IT tasks to justify their cost."

The practical consequence: the internal IT person is usually an ally rather than an obstacle, because the work you are proposing is work they cannot do and are not resourced to do. Selling around them is a mistake; selling *with* them is usually available.

## What does an SMB actually care about when choosing technology?

Not price first. This is the most counter-intuitive finding in Microsoft's SMB research and the most useful one. Asked which factors are important when selecting a new technology solution, SMBs ranked **reliability at 41%** and **security at 40%** top, followed by efficiency and customer/technical support at 36% each, ease of use at 35%, value for money and range of functionalities at 34% each, and **cost at 30%** — near the bottom of the list.

Microsoft's own interpretation is worth quoting: "The popularity of reliability suggests that SMBs want technologies that 'just work' without excessive complications," and on security, "SMBs see significant threats to their business and data." The study concludes that "cost and value for money are not critical when SMBs are selecting technologies," being "selected by fewer than a third of SMBs."

This does not mean price is irrelevant — see the barriers section below, where cost dominates. It means price is a barrier to *adoption*, not a criterion for *selection*. A pitch that leads on cheapness is answering a question the buyer did not ask.

## What stops an SMB from adopting new technology?

Cost — but a specific kind of cost. The study identifies **the cost of new solutions as the primary barrier at 47%**, "by far the most common challenge to SMBs during technology adoption." Crucially it specifies which cost: "SMBs explicitly specify high ongoing costs of new technologies as an inhibitor while less than half of SMBs are concerned about the upfront costs."

The remaining barriers cluster tightly, each cited by roughly a third to 38% of respondents: difficulty comparing solutions and making decisions, technical concerns, and uncertainty over the business impact, with difficulty researching or finding information close behind and **resistance to change lowest at 29%**. The study characterises the uncertainty group vividly — these SMBs "see adopting new technologies as a 'leap of faith'." It also notes that "half of SMBs fear missing out on better alternatives."

Read together, the picture is a buyer who is not resistant to change and not primarily worried about the cheque they write on day one, but who is worried about what this commits them to forever and cannot easily tell whether they are choosing well.

## How is an SMB buyer different from an enterprise buyer?

Four differences matter operationally. First, **committee size**: an enterprise decision involves a procurement function, an architecture review and a security review; an SMB decision often involves one executive who also runs operations. Second, **evaluation depth**: an enterprise runs a structured RFP; an SMB struggles to compare options at all, with difficulty comparing solutions cited as a top-three barrier.

Third, **what counts as proof**: an enterprise wants a reference architecture and a pilot; an SMB wants evidence it "just works," which is why reliability outranks everything else. Fourth, **the cost frame**: an enterprise negotiates on total contract value; an SMB is anxious about the recurring monthly obligation, which is why ongoing cost worries them more than upfront cost.

There is also a structural difference in who the vendor is to them. Microsoft's research frames SMBs as behaving "more akin to consumers" in their decision-making. Enterprise buyers manage vendors; SMB buyers want to stop thinking about the problem.

## What do SMBs want from a technology partner?

They want proactivity above everything. Asked to rank the most important services from a technology partner over the following two to three years, SMBs put **"suggest new technology to help my business" first at 51%**, followed by **"upgrade security to cover new problems" at 47%**, **"provide contingency and business continuity planning" at 43%**, **"provide technical support to employees working from home" at 41%**, "offer complementary products/services" at 40%, and "offer flexible payment terms" and "provide accessible customer support" at 39% each.

Microsoft's framing is that the partner role "is becoming a more strategic one," with SMBs looking for partners "to proactively suggest new solutions and help to shape their technology strategy." The study also found **31% of SMBs prefer to buy technology products and services through MSPs and CSPs** — the most common channel preference — because "many SMBs do not have the capacity or the requirement to onboard IT specialists."

The uncomfortable implication for a reactive, ticket-driven partner is that the single most valued service is the one they are structurally least likely to deliver. A partner who only responds to requests is failing the number-one stated expectation of the segment.

## Currency warning

All figures here are from one study: fieldwork April–May 2022, n=3,065 SMBs with 1–300 employees, across ten markets (Australia, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Mexico, South Africa, UK, USA), in the top five technology-spending verticals per country. Commissioned by Microsoft, prepared by Analysys Mason, published September 2022.

The data is roughly four years old at retrieval and predates the generative-AI wave, so attitudes to AI, and possibly to security spend, will have moved. Some findings — particularly the emphasis on support for employees working from home — reflect the immediate post-pandemic period. No newer Microsoft-published SMB attitudes study was located during research. Use these as directional insight into buyer behaviour rather than as current statistics, and attribute them to the 2022 study when quoting.
