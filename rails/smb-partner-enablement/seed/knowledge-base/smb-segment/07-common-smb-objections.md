# Common SMB objections and grounded responses

> Source: https://blogs.microsoft.com/wp-content/uploads/prod/2022/09/Microsoft-Small-and-Medium-Sized-Business-Voice-and-Attitudes-to-Technology-Study-2022.pdf · https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2025/10/16/mddr-2025/ · https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/security-insider/threat-landscape/microsoft-digital-defense-report-2025 · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-business/mdb-overview
> As of: 2025-10 · Retrieved: 2026-08-14

## Objection: "It's too expensive"

Lead by separating the two costs, because Microsoft's own SMB research shows the customer is worried about only one of them. The study found the cost of new solutions is the primary barrier to adoption at 47%, and specified which cost: SMBs "explicitly specify high ongoing costs of new technologies as an inhibitor while less than half of SMBs are concerned about the upfront costs." The anxiety is about the permanent monthly obligation, not the first invoice.

So do not respond with a discount. Respond by naming what the monthly line replaces. Most SMBs are already paying, in some combination, for email hosting, file storage, a video conferencing tool, an antivirus product, a backup service and ad-hoc IT callouts. Putting those on one line and showing the net change reframes the conversation from "a new cost" to "a consolidation." Where the net change is genuinely upward, say so and attach it to a specific risk or capability rather than defending the number in the abstract.

There is a useful supporting fact from the same study: when SMBs are *selecting* technology, cost ranks near the bottom of their criteria at 30%, well behind reliability at 41% and security at 40%. Price dominates the adoption decision but not the selection decision, which means a cheaper proposal rarely wins on its own.

## Objection: "We already pay for Google Workspace"

Do not open by attacking the productivity suite, because for a business that is happy with mail and docs you will lose that argument and it is not the argument worth having. Open by asking what sits around it: what handles endpoint protection, what enrols and manages the laptops and phones, what enforces conditional access, and what happens when someone leaves. In most small businesses those are separate products with separate bills, or they are absent.

The grounded Microsoft claim is what Microsoft 365 Business Premium includes rather than what a competitor lacks. Microsoft documents that Business Premium includes Microsoft Defender for Business, an endpoint security solution "designed for small and medium sized business up to 300 users" that "offers protection from ransomware, malware, phishing, and other threats on devices," bringing capabilities from Defender for Endpoint Plan 1, several from Plan 2, and some unique to smaller businesses. Business Premium adds further security and productivity capability on top of that.

The honest framing is a consolidation argument, not a features table: one vendor, one identity, one management surface, one bill, one place to call. Where the customer's real answer is "the team likes Google," accept it and check whether a security and device-management layer is nonetheless missing — that gap is worth solving whether or not the suite ever changes.

## Objection: "We're too small for AI"

The response is not a bigger vision, it is a smaller one. Ask what the business does repeatedly that nobody enjoys — drafting quotes, chasing invoices, writing the same customer email, summarising a meeting nobody had time to attend, finding the version of the document that is actually current. Those are the tasks a small team feels most acutely because there is no one to delegate them to. A 20-person business does not have less need for time back than a 2,000-person business; it has less slack.

Microsoft's SMB research supports the framing that the barrier is uncertainty rather than scale. Roughly a third to 38% of SMBs cited uncertainty over the business impact of new technology as an adoption barrier, and the study describes this group as seeing adoption "as a 'leap of faith'." The same research found that difficulty comparing solutions and making decisions was an equally large barrier. Neither of those is solved by a larger claim; both are solved by a small, bounded, reversible first step with a named person and a visible result.

Note also which SMBs are growing. The study found early adopters of technology were about twice as likely to achieve high revenue growth and 1.9 times more likely to have achieved some of their business objectives. That is correlation, not proof of cause, and should be presented as such — but "the businesses like yours that move early tend to be the ones growing" is a fair and citable observation.

## Objection: "Nobody would bother attacking a business our size"

This is the most confidently held and least accurate belief in the segment, and the counter-argument should be about economics rather than fear. Attacks are not targeted at businesses because they are valuable; they are automated and targeted at businesses because they are reachable. Microsoft's Digital Defense Report 2025 found that more than 52% of cyberattacks with known motives were driven by extortion or ransomware, and that in 80% of the incidents Microsoft investigated, attackers were seeking to steal data. Neither of those motives requires the victim to be large — extortion works on any business that cannot operate without its data.

Microsoft's report is also explicit that thin resourcing is itself an attractor, describing organisations targeted because they "store sensitive data or have tight cybersecurity budgets with limited incident response capabilities, often resulting in outdated software." That is a description of most small businesses, and it inverts the objection: being small is the reason to expect attention, not the reason to be overlooked.

The most useful single statistic is the remedy rather than the threat. Microsoft reports that over 97% of identity attacks are password attacks, and that multifactor authentication can block over 99% of identity-based attacks. That gives you a proportionate, cheap, same-week first action rather than a project.

## Objection: "We've never had a problem, so what we have now is working"

Absence of a known incident is weak evidence, and the way to say so without being condescending is to ask how they would know. Most small businesses have no endpoint detection, no log retention and no alerting, so the honest answer is usually that a compromise would be discovered when something visibly broke or when a customer complained. Microsoft's Digital Defense Report 2025 notes that identity-based attacks surged 32% in the first half of 2025, so the base rate is moving even where a specific business's experience has not.

The productive move is to convert this into a diagnostic rather than a debate. Offer to check three concrete things: whether multifactor authentication is enforced for every user including admins, whether company data can be wiped from a lost or stolen personal phone, and whether anyone would be alerted if a mailbox started forwarding externally. These are answerable in an afternoon and the answers are usually uncomfortable enough to move the conversation without any argument from you.

Microsoft's own SMB research suggests the appetite is already there even where the urgency is not: 47% of SMBs named "upgrade security to cover new problems" as one of the most important services they want from a technology partner, second only to proactive technology suggestions.

## Objection: "We don't have anyone to manage this"

Take this one at face value, because it is usually true and it is the strongest opening a partner gets. Microsoft's SMB study found that although 77% of SMBs have some internal IT staff, those staff "rarely specialize in areas that are essential to the adoption of new technology, such as platform development or cyber security," and that 45% of SMBs already rely on external consultants or partners for exactly this reason. Among the smallest businesses, only 45% have dedicated full-time internal IT staff at all.

So the answer is not "it manages itself." The answer is that you manage it, and that this is a normal and common arrangement rather than an admission of failure — 31% of SMBs already name MSPs and CSPs as their preferred route for buying and managing technology, the most common single preference in the study.

It is worth adding that Microsoft has built for this. Defender for Business is documented as offering "wizard-driven configuration and default security policies that are designed to help protect your company's devices from day one," with the explicit claim that "no specialized knowledge is required," and it integrates with Microsoft 365 Lighthouse for CSPs and with RMM and PSA tooling for MSPs — meaning your ability to run it across many customers is a designed-in capability, not a workaround.

## Objection: "We looked at this before and it was too disruptive"

Acknowledge the experience rather than disputing it, then narrow the scope. Microsoft's research found resistance to change was the *lowest* of the named adoption barriers at 29%, well behind cost and decision-difficulty — which suggests that what is usually described as resistance to change is more often unresolved uncertainty about impact and about how to compare options. Treated as a change-management problem it stalls; treated as a scoping problem it moves.

Practically, that means proposing the smallest coherent unit of value rather than the full estate: one workload, one department, one clearly reversible step, with the success criteria agreed before you start. This also aligns with what SMBs say they want from partners — the top-ranked service at 51% was "suggest new technology to help my business," which is a request for a recommendation with a point of view, not a menu of options for them to evaluate.

## Currency warning

The buyer-attitude figures here (adoption barriers, selection criteria, partner expectations, IT staffing) come from the Microsoft-commissioned Analysys Mason SMB voice and attitudes study, fieldwork April–May 2022, n=3,065 SMBs of 1–300 employees across ten markets. That data predates the generative-AI product cycle and is roughly four years old at retrieval; treat it as directional.

The threat figures come from the Microsoft Digital Defense Report 2025, published 16 October 2025 — Microsoft publishes this annually, so check for a newer edition before quoting. Product capability statements reflect Microsoft Learn documentation dated 2025-08-27 for Defender for Business; SKU contents and seat limits change. This file deliberately contains no pricing, because published list prices move frequently and vary by market — verify current pricing on microsoft.com for the customer's region before using a number.
