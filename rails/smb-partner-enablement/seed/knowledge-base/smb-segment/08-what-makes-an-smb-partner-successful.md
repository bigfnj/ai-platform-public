# What makes an SMB Microsoft partner successful?

> Source: https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/04/21/accelerating-frontier-transformation-with-microsoft-partners/ · https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2025/03/24/microsoft-at-50-the-journey-and-future-of-the-partner-ecosystem/ · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/membership/partner-capability-score · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-business/mdb-partners · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/lighthouse/m365-lighthouse-overview · https://tei.forrester.com/go/microsoft/SecurityTEI2024/ · https://pulse.microsoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/IDC-EUR149732822-Microsoft-Partner-Economic-Value-eBook.pdf

## Why does recurring revenue decide whether an SMB practice works?

Because SMB deal sizes are too small to sustain a business built on one-off transactions. A partner selling 40 seats at a time on project margin has to win an enormous number of deals to stand still, and every one of them costs acquisition effort. A partner earning a monthly managed-services fee on those same 40 seats has revenue that arrives whether or not they closed anything that month.

Microsoft's own data on its channel points the same way. A Microsoft blog dated April 2026 states that "**60% of CSP partner revenue is now tied to value-added services**, with licensing acting as the entry point to broader, services-led engagements." The framing there is worth taking literally: licensing is the entry point, not the business. A partner whose revenue is mostly licence resale is running the less profitable 40% of the model.

Microsoft describes the target shape directly, noting that in "the small and medium business space (SMB), Microsoft partners offer end-to-end capability through **managed services offerings and solutions packaged into repeatable motions**, tailored to this customer segment." The two operative words are *managed* and *repeatable*.

## How much does a partner earn per dollar of Microsoft revenue?

Microsoft publishes an ecosystem multiplier sourced to IDC. Its Chief Partner Officer wrote in March 2025: "According to IDC, **for every $1 of Microsoft revenue, services partners earn $8.45, and software partners earn $10.93.**" The footnote attributes this to IDC's study "Microsoft Partners: Driving Economic Value and AI Maturity." Note that the underlying IDC document is not freely retrievable, so this is verifiable as a Microsoft-published claim citing IDC rather than as a document you can hand a customer.

An earlier IDC eBook sponsored by Microsoft, which is publicly downloadable, gives the same multiplier broken out by business model for a European sample: "services-led partners in Europe make $5.75 and software-led partners make $7.86… In comparison, **resale-led partners in Europe make $2.21** in economic impact." That study surveyed 288 European partners as part of a larger global survey of 774, and is dated October 2022 — so it is both older and narrower in scope, and should be labelled as the European cut rather than presented as global.

The shape of the finding is what matters and it is consistent across both: resale earns the least, services earn multiples more, and software or IP earns the most. A partner choosing where to invest has that answer published.

## What are the actual margins by business model?

The same publicly downloadable IDC study reports gross margin by partner business model for its European sample: **resale-led 18%, services-led 26%, software-led 32%**. It also found margin rising with Microsoft concentration — partners drawing 1–25% of revenue from Microsoft reported 14% gross margin, rising through 22% and 28% to **30% for partners drawing more than 75% of revenue from Microsoft**.

That second finding is counter-intuitive and worth sitting with. Diversifying away from Microsoft did not protect margin in this sample; specialising deeply improved it. The mechanism is presumably repeatability — a partner doing one vendor's work over and over gets better and faster at it.

Expected revenue growth followed the same ordering, with software-led partners reporting far higher growth expectations than services-led or resale-led. Treat all of these as directional European data from 2022 rather than current global figures, but note that they point the same direction as Microsoft's own more recent statement that 60% of CSP partner revenue is now tied to value-added services.

## What is the specific managed-services opportunity in SMB security?

A Forrester Total Economic Impact study commissioned by Microsoft and published July 2024 is the only partner-opportunity study located with a genuine SMB cut, and its findings are unusually direct. It reported SMB partner expected revenue of **$17.70 per user per month, growing 19% year on year**, and — critically — that **managed services grew 53% for SMB customers versus 17% for enterprise customers**.

That gap is the whole argument for an SMB managed-services practice in one number. Managed services are growing more than three times faster in SMB than in enterprise, because SMB customers have no realistic alternative.

The study also captured a partner quote that describes the standard SMB motion precisely: "**We lead with Microsoft 365 Business Premium because of the comprehensive suite of security tools. Ninety percent of our customers buy this SKU.**" Note that companion Forrester studies covering Modern Work report much higher per-user figures, but those are explicitly normalised to enterprise customers — do not blend the numbers across studies, segments or years.

## What does "repeatable" actually mean for an SMB practice?

It means the same deployment, delivered the same way, to customer after customer — and it is the single biggest determinant of whether an SMB practice is profitable. Enterprise partners can afford bespoke architecture because the deal funds it. At SMB deal sizes, every hour of custom design destroys the margin.

The practical test is whether you can describe your offer as a fixed package with a fixed price and a fixed delivery runbook. A partner who quotes each customer from scratch is running a consultancy at SMB prices, which does not work. A partner with three standard packages — say a secured productivity baseline, a security uplift, and a Business Central deployment — is running a product business with services attached.

Microsoft builds toward this. Microsoft 365 Lighthouse ships "a default SMB security baseline that prescribes best practices targeted to small- and medium-sized business tenants" and provides "tenant deployment journeys so technicians can follow a consistent set of steps to secure and configure customer tenants." That is Microsoft supplying the standard configuration so the partner does not have to invent one per customer.

## What tooling lets a small partner manage many customers?

The multi-tenant tooling is the difference between a practice that scales and one that caps out at the number of customers a technician can hold in their head. **Microsoft 365 Lighthouse** is the central piece: Microsoft describes it as helping "Managed Service Providers (MSPs) grow their business and deliver services to customers at scale from a single portal," providing "multi-tenant views across all customer environments" so engineers "can scale the management of their customers, focus on what's most important, quickly find and investigate risks, and take action."

Two facts about Lighthouse matter commercially. It is free — "No additional costs are associated with using Lighthouse to manage Microsoft 365 services and connected devices" — and it is **CSP-gated**: "Lighthouse is available to partners enrolled in the Cloud Solution Provider (CSP) program, including both Direct-Bill and Indirect Resellers." That is a concrete reason to be in CSP beyond the margin.

Lighthouse also has a sales function that partners routinely ignore. Microsoft describes it as delivering "AI-driven insights… proactive, actionable, and personalized recommendations to help acquire new customers, improve customer retention, and expand business with premium offers." On the security side, Microsoft Defender for Business integrates with Lighthouse for CSPs and with **RMM tools and PSA software** for MSPs, so endpoint security can be run through the tooling a managed-services practice already uses.

## Why is security the most reliable practice to build in SMB?

Because it is the one thing SMB customers say they want, cannot do themselves, and will pay monthly for. Microsoft's commissioned SMB research found that "upgrade security to cover new problems" ranked second among the most important services SMBs want from a technology partner, at 47%, and that internal IT staff "rarely specialize in areas that are essential to the adoption of new technology, such as platform development or **cyber security**."

The product economics work too. Microsoft 365 Business Premium bundles the security stack into a licence the customer is already buying, and Microsoft has since added SMB-scoped Defender and Purview suites on top of it — giving a partner a second and third sale into the same 300-seat customer without leaving SMB licensing.

Crucially, security is inherently a *managed* service rather than a project. Endpoint detection generates alerts that someone must triage; posture drifts and must be re-baselined; new devices arrive. Microsoft's design assumption is that this is the partner's job, and Defender for Business is explicitly built so it can be — "no specialized knowledge is required," with "wizard-driven configuration and default security policies." That lowers the bar for a small partner to start a security practice without hiring a security specialist first.

## What sequence should a partner sell in?

Migration and consolidation first, security second, AI third — and Microsoft's own targeting data supports that order rather than partner preference. Partner Center's CloudAscent documentation reports that "**80% of Copilot subscriptions or licenses are sold within Cohorts 4 and 5**," the cohorts describing customers who already hold ten or more productivity bundles and have at least 50% security-suite adoption.

At the other end, CloudAscent's Cohort 1 is defined as "Customers without an active Microsoft 365 subscription… on-premises deployments, or competitive displacement." Those customers are migration prospects, not Copilot prospects, and pitching AI into them is selling against the pattern in Microsoft's own data.

This gives a partner a genuine account-development ladder rather than a product list. Land the customer on Microsoft 365, consolidate their estate, attach Business Premium and run the security practice, and the AI conversation becomes available on its own terms — with a customer whose estate is now actually ready for it. Each rung raises the monthly recurring value of the same account, which is how SMB practices grow without proportionally growing customer count.

## Is there a separate, easier SMB path to a Solutions Partner designation?

Yes, and it is probably the single most actionable fact in this file. Microsoft documents that "There are two distinct attainment paths to earn a Solutions Partner designation in the Data & AI, Digital & App Innovation, Infrastructure, Modern work, and Business applications areas" — an **Enterprise** path and a **Small and medium business (SMB)** path. For Modern Work and Security, Microsoft states it evaluates a partner on both paths and takes "the highest of the two partner capability scores towards qualifying for the designation," so an SMB partner is not penalised for being scored twice.

The SMB path is materially easier across every category. In Modern Work, an eligible tenant on the SMB track needs "paid licenses between 11 to 300" against more than 300 for Enterprise, and the skilling requirement drops from four intermediate plus two advanced certified individuals to **two intermediate plus one advanced**. In Security, the eligible tenant floor is lower still at 5–300 paid licences, and net customer adds required for full points halves from ten customers to **five**. In Business Applications, the intermediate certification requirement drops from twenty people to **five**. In Azure, the eligible-customer threshold falls from USD 1,000 to USD 500 of Azure Consumed Revenue.

A designation is worth pursuing partly for its own benefits and partly because Microsoft states plainly that "A Solutions Partner designation is a prerequisite to earn a specialization."

## Why does the SMB designation track effectively require CSP?

Because of which partner-of-record associations each track accepts. On the SMB track for Modern Work, valid associations are **CPOR, CSP Tier 1 and CSP Tier 2**. On the Enterprise track, they are CPOR and DPOR. Since DPOR attaches to Enterprise Agreement subscriptions and CSP Tier associations attach to CSP transactions, a partner transacting through CSP can realistically score on the SMB path and largely cannot score on the Enterprise path.

This is Microsoft hard-wiring the CSP motion into SMB designation attainment rather than merely encouraging it, and it aligns with Microsoft's description of CSP as its "hero motion" for the segment. The practical consequence for a partner deciding whether to transact through CSP is that the decision affects far more than margin — it determines whether your customer base is even measurable for a designation.

Microsoft has also been widening SMB-track access. A July 2026 announcement removed the Azure Consumed Revenue threshold for SMB-track eligibility across the Cloud & AI Platforms solution paths, stating that "partners with at least 80% of their customer base in small, medium, and corporate-corporate (SMC-C) and SMB segments can qualify for the SMB track" and that this "creates a simpler path for SMB-focused partners to attain a Solutions Partner designation."

## Is there an SMB-specific specialization?

One, and it sits on Business Central. The **Small and Midsize Business Management** specialization has Solutions Partner for Business Applications as its prerequisite and — unusually — requires **no third-party audit and no customer references**, which removes the two most expensive barriers partners normally face. Microsoft lowered its bar in 2026, reducing "the required MAU growth threshold… from 35% to 15%, while the deployment requirement has increased from 10 to 15 deployments."

Other specializations relevant to an SMB practice without being SMB-named include Secure AI Productivity, Modernize Endpoints, Calling for Microsoft Teams, Microsoft 365 Copilot, and the four security specializations covering cloud security, identity and access management, data security and threat protection. Note that those four moved to a partner-funded third-party audit every two years as of mid-2026, which is a real cost line to plan for.

Two structural points worth knowing: there is **no SMB-specific designation** and no SMB-specific benefits bundle — benefits attach to the six solution pathways and to specializations. And Azure Expert MSP, the formal audited MSP program, is aimed at scaled managed-service providers rather than typical SMB practices.

## Does CSP direct-bill status require a managed-services practice?

Effectively yes, and this is a useful forcing function. Microsoft's published direct-bill requirements include the obligation to "**Demonstrate that you provide at least one managed service, IP service, or customer solution application**," alongside at least twelve prior months as an authorised indirect reseller, at least USD one million in CSP trailing-twelve-month transactional revenue, a passing capabilities assessment, at least one Solutions Partner designation, and purchase of an Advanced or Premier Support for Partners plan. The capabilities assessment itself names "Managed services: A well-established practice to complement Microsoft cloud products" as an assessed area.

Most SMB partners will and should remain **indirect resellers**, where requirements are light — membership, the ability to sign agreements, and "A partnership with an authorized distributor," with no Solutions Partner designation required. Microsoft's own description of the split is that "The Distributor handles billing with Microsoft; the reseller owns the customer relationship."

Note two economic consequences of that choice. Indirect resellers do not draw CSP margin from Microsoft directly — it reaches them via the distributor — and Microsoft states there is "no direct 'conversion' from Indirect Reseller to direct bill." Also relevant to any Azure practice: Partner Earned Credit is explicitly tied to managed services, described by Microsoft as available to "Billing partners who provide this 24 X 7 cloud-operations management activity," which means the Azure margin story and the managed-services story are the same story.

## How does a partner get Microsoft to invest in them?

By being visible in the places Microsoft looks, and by being in the motion Microsoft has funded. Microsoft's Chief Partner Officer has stated that Microsoft "dedicated **70% of our total incentive spend** to partners that serve the Small, Medium Enterprises and Channel (SME&C) segment," and described the Cloud Solution Provider program in the same piece: "**CSP is our hero motion** that enables those partners to drive this business transformation," with CSP partners characterised as "the trusted advisors who serve these customers."

Visibility is more mechanical than partners assume. Microsoft's Partner Center guidance is that "the most important step you can take to start getting sales leads from us is to create a business profile that showcases your company's offerings, solutions, and expertise," and that responsiveness compounds — respond quickly and "we'll increase your visibility in future partner search results progressively," while accepting poorly-fitting deals "won't improve your search results."

Beyond that, sharing pipeline through Partner Center even where no Microsoft help is needed — the partner-led deal type — keeps a partner's activity visible to Microsoft without consuming seller attention that a small deal would not attract anyway.

## What does the SMB customer actually want from you?

Proactivity, and it is the single clearest finding in Microsoft's SMB research. Asked to rank the most important services from a technology partner, SMBs put "**suggest new technology to help my business**" first at **51%** — ahead of security, ahead of continuity planning, ahead of support. Microsoft's own summary is that the partner role "is becoming a more strategic one," with SMBs looking to partners "to proactively suggest new solutions and help to shape their technology strategy."

This is uncomfortable for the typical SMB partner, because a break-fix or ticket-driven operation is structurally incapable of delivering the top-ranked thing customers want. Nobody in that model is paid to think about the customer's business between incidents.

The operational fix is a scheduled account review — quarterly, with an agenda, a view of what has changed in the customer's business, and one recommendation. That single ritual converts a support vendor into the "trusted advisor" role Microsoft's research says SMBs are looking for, and it is also where expansion pipeline comes from. In MCEM terms it is stage 5 feeding stage 1, and it is the mechanism by which a partner grows revenue per customer instead of only growing customer count.

## What does Microsoft publish to help build the practice?

Microsoft maintains a partner-facing page for exactly this, and its framing of the demand side is worth quoting to a sceptical practice owner: "Small and medium-sized businesses recognize that security is important, but they often **don't have the capacity or expertise to have a dedicated security operations team**. These customers often need help with setup and configuration, managing security for their devices and network, and addressing alerts or detected threats. **Microsoft partners can help.**"

The named resources include a Microsoft 365 Business Premium partner playbook and readiness series, described as "practical guidance on building a profitable managed services practice" covering successful managed-service offering examples, technical enablement checklists, and sales enablement aids; a Defender for Business partner kit; a practical guide and checklist for SMB security; and documentation for integrating endpoint security with RMM and PSA tooling.

Two cautions. Microsoft's `aka.ms` short links into this material redirect frequently and some historical partner-blog posts on building a Business Premium managed-services practice now dead-end at microsoft.com — do not cite those. And most of the downloadable partner assets sit behind Partner Center sign-in, so the public pages describe them rather than deliver them.

## Currency warning

**Microsoft does not publish CSP margin rates, incentive rates, or Partner Earned Credit rates.** They are gated behind Partner Center sign-in, and the Partner Economics Modeler documentation is explicitly marked Microsoft Confidential. Percentages that appear in calculation tables on Microsoft Learn pages — such as a "20% base margin" or "15% PEC" — are **worked arithmetic examples, not published rates**, and must never be quoted as rates. Growth margins were documented as sandbox-only and not yet in production as of August 2026.

Program mechanics here change monthly and several changed recently. Solutions Partner badges were renamed on 2026-07-22 to three badges (Cloud & AI Platforms, AI Business Solutions, Security) over the same six unchanged solution pathways; SMB track eligibility was expanded 2026-07-31, and at retrieval the Azure designation page still showed the superseded threshold — which of the two governs is not resolvable from public sources. Security specializations moved to a partner-funded third-party audit as of 2026-07-30. Note also that Microsoft Inspire no longer exists; it was replaced by MCAPS Start for Partners. Re-verify at https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/announcements/ before relying on any threshold.

The Forrester SMB security figures ($17.70 per user per month, 53% managed-services growth) are from a Microsoft-commissioned TEI study dated July 2024; do not blend them with the enterprise-scoped Modern Work TEI figures.
