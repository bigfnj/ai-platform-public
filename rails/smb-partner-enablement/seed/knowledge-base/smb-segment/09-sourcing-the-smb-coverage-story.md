# Sourcing the SMB coverage story — which citations hold, and which numbers to refuse

> Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/789019/000095017025100235/msft-20250630.htm (FY2025 10-K) · https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/789019/000119312526323660/msft-20260630.htm (FY2026 10-K) · https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2025/03/24/microsoft-at-50-the-journey-and-future-of-the-partner-ecosystem/
> As of: 2026-08 · Retrieved: 2026-08-14

## Why does it matter which year's 10-K I cite for the channel model?

Because the detail was deleted. Microsoft's **FY2025 10-K** (filed 30 July 2025) describes the
route to market granularly, naming the reseller types and mapping them to customer size: "each
type of reselling partner may reach organizations of all sizes, LSPs are primarily engaged with
large organizations, distributors resell primarily to VARs, and **VARs typically reach small and
medium organizations**," and states that "**Microsoft Cloud Solution Provider is our main partner
program for reselling cloud services**."

The **FY2026 10-K** (filed 29 July 2026) rewrote and shortened that section into generic
language. A full-text search of the FY2026 filing returns **zero occurrences** of "LSP",
"value-added reseller", "Cloud Solution Provider", "field sales", or "small, medium, and
corporate". If you cite the granular channel mapping, cite **FY2025 or FY2024** — the claim is
still well-evidenced, but not in the newest filing. Anyone checking your work against the
current 10-K will not find it, so name the year.

## Does Microsoft publish the cost economics of partner-led versus direct coverage?

No, and this is worth stating plainly because the question invites invention. A full-text search
of both the FY2025 and FY2026 10-Ks returns **zero occurrences** of "cost of sale", "cost to
serve", or "partner-led". Microsoft reports **Sales and marketing as a single consolidated
expense line** with no breakdown by channel, route to market, or customer segment.

So Microsoft publicly describes *that* it reaches SMB largely through partners and indirect
motions, and publicly commits incentive dollars to that channel — but it publishes no
cost-per-sale, cost-per-touch, customer-acquisition-cost, or direct-versus-partner comparison.
Any such figure is inference. Present it as reasoning, never as a Microsoft number.

## Which SMB coverage figures should I refuse to use?

These circulate widely and none trace to a citable primary source. Refusing them is the whole
point of this collection.

The cluster **"SMC & Digital = 7,000 people, 90% of Microsoft's customers, $60B revenue, 90,000+
partners, 36,000 corporate customers"** traces only to channel-influencer LinkedIn posts and
podcasts. There is no Microsoft-published source. The **"$50,000 ACV minimum to make direct sales
profitable"** traces to a boutique consultancy blog with no primary research. The **"a typical
Microsoft seller manages 400+ accounts versus 25–40 for an MSP"** traces to MSP-vendor marketing.
A family of statistics — "72% say partner CAC is lower", "partner-sourced deals close 46%
faster", "Gartner says indirect costs add 8–12%" — appears across content-marketing sites that
name-drop Gartner or Forrester without linking any retrievable document.

Treat all of the above as unusable. They are precisely the kind of confident, quotable number a
partner would repeat in front of a customer, and none of them survive a source check.

## Which sources DO hold for the coverage story?

Four, in descending strength. The **FY2025 10-K** is the best primary source for the channel
model, including the frequently-missed line that "Microsoft offers direct sales programs targeted
to reach small, medium, and corporate customers, **in addition to** those offered through the
reseller channel" — SMB is not purely partner-only. **Amy Hood on the FY25 Q2 earnings call (29
January 2025)** ties the segment to route-to-market: the "scale motions… primarily, these are
customers we reach through partners and through more indirect methods of selling." The **"Microsoft
at 50" partner blog (24 March 2025)** by the Chief Partner Officer supplies "Microsoft has always
been a partner-led company", CSP as the "hero motion for small and medium enterprises", the
**$661 billion SME&C TAM**, and **70% of total incentive spend dedicated to SME&C partners** —
the strongest public evidence that Microsoft *funds* SMB coverage through the channel. **Partner
Center announcements** give durable, dated segment terminology.

## What about the "95% of Microsoft revenue flows through partners" statistic?

It is real and Microsoft-published, but it is **from February 2019** — a blog post by the then-CVP
of One Commercial Partner. Seven years on, the organisation (One Commercial Partner became
SME&C), the programs, and the EA/LSP model have all changed materially. No newer Microsoft
restatement of a partner-revenue percentage could be located.

Use it if you must, but date-stamp it every time. An undated "95% of Microsoft's revenue goes
through partners" presented as current is the kind of claim that gets corrected in front of a
customer.

## Which channel-industry links are already dead?

Two traps for anyone refreshing this collection. **Channel Futures has been sunset** — its URLs
now redirect to a Channel Dive welcome notice, so no `channelfutures.com` link should be cited.
And **Canalys was absorbed into Omdia/Informa**; its newsroom URLs 301-redirect away from the
original articles, so the widely-quoted "partners deliver more than 70% of a $4.7 trillion IT
market" figure should be cited to the trade-press coverage that reported it rather than to the
vanished Canalys release.

## Currency warning

Everything here is a citation-hygiene note, and citation hygiene decays. 10-K language changes
annually — the FY2026 rewrite documented above is the proof. Executive blog posts get
unpublished, and channel-media domains are being consolidated rapidly. Re-verify any link in this
file before relying on it, and re-check whether the newest 10-K has restored or further removed
the channel detail.
