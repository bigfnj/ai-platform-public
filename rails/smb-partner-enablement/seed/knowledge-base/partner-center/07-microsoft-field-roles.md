# Working with Microsoft's field — which roles a partner meets, and what is not publicly documented

> Source: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/referrals/manage-co-sell-opportunities · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/referrals/manage-leads · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/referrals/services-co-sell · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/referrals/co-sell-configure · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/benefits/technical-benefits · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/earnings/earnings · https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/incentives/mci-engagements-customers

## What can and cannot be verified about Microsoft's field roles

This is the topic where publicly available Microsoft documentation is thinnest, so the honest scope needs stating up front. Microsoft Learn documents the *system* a partner interacts with — Microsoft sellers, MSX, account coverage, Partner Technical Consultants, response windows — but it does **not** publish a definition of the Partner Development Manager (PDM) role, the criteria for being a "managed" partner, or Microsoft's field coverage model. Microsoft's own Q&A responses on how to get a PDM assigned redirect the asker to the Microsoft Partner Community and Partner Center Support rather than to any documented process. Everything below that is stated as fact comes from Microsoft Learn; where something could not be verified, that is said explicitly rather than filled in.

## What does a Partner Development Manager (PDM) do?

**This could not be verified from public Microsoft documentation.** The term appears in Microsoft partner blogs, in Microsoft job postings, and in partner-community discussion, but Microsoft Learn carries no article defining the PDM role, its responsibilities, or which partners are assigned one. A Microsoft Q&A thread asking exactly this — "What procedure is to be followed to become a managed partner and get a partner development manager assigned?" — was answered by redirecting the partner to the Microsoft Partner Community and Partner Center Support, with no procedural steps given. Any figure a partner has heard about how many partners get a PDM, or what threshold triggers assignment, should be treated as unverified. The correct answer to a partner asking about PDM coverage is to point them at Partner Center Support and the Microsoft Partner Community, which is where Microsoft itself points.

## Which Microsoft people does a partner actually encounter through Partner Center?

Four contact surfaces are documented. **Microsoft sellers** appear on co-sell opportunities — they can send opportunities to a partner (inbound), accept or decline a partner's invitation (outbound), and mark a deal won. **Microsoft SMC-Corporate sellers** originate Joint Planning leads, which Microsoft describes as leads "given to partners" that "help align customer territory planning and support co-selling success." **Microsoft sellers working in MSX** create Qualified leads and SMB Opportunities and nominate the partner contact who will receive them. **Partner Technical Consultants** deliver Technical Presales and Deployment consultations. Beyond these, Partner Center exposes Microsoft's **deal validation team** and **exception council** as decision-making bodies rather than named individuals.

## What is MSX and why does it matter to a partner?

MSX is Microsoft Sales Experience, Microsoft's internal CRM, and it is the system on the other side of several Partner Center flows. Microsoft's documentation references it in three places relevant to partners. First, for **Qualified leads and SMB Opportunities**, the recipient at the partner is "the contact who was added on the lead in MSX" — a Microsoft seller chooses who at your company gets the lead, at the moment they create it. Second, for **variable pay MCI engagements**, a customer can *only* be nominated using an **MSX opportunity ID**, and the proposed payout amount is calculated from that ID plus the hours you specify. Third, some partner activity engagements only allow customer nomination by MSX opportunity ID at all. In each case, the partner is dependent on a Microsoft seller having created the corresponding MSX record.

## Does a Microsoft seller have to help me if I ask?

No — participation is explicitly discretionary and time-boxed. Microsoft's wording is that in a co-sell "a Microsoft seller can potentially help closing the deal, although their participation isn't guaranteed," and that "Microsoft sales representatives have a 14-day window to decide whether they'll participate in the deal." You can see the outcome on the **Microsoft** card below your deal details. If the request is declined, Microsoft seller details are not shown. If a seller declined by accident, the documented remedy is to re-create the opportunity and invite Microsoft again. The mirror-image rule applies to you: if you do not respond to an *inbound* opportunity within 14 days it is archived as expired, and Microsoft or the sending partner is notified.

## Why does Microsoft sometimes have no seller for my customer?

Because customer coverage, not partner status, determines seller availability. Partner Center splits customer accounts into **Microsoft Managed** (Microsoft's sales team has an existing relationship with the company), **Microsoft Unmanaged** (not managed by Microsoft), and **Other** (accounts from the Moody's database, where managed status cannot be confirmed until Microsoft's internal account matching runs). The two documented decline reasons map straight onto this: *"No corresponding Microsoft customer record could be found"* means Microsoft has no relationship with the customer, and *"No Microsoft seller directly manages this customer"* means Microsoft does not manage them. This is the structural reality of the SMB segment — smaller customers are frequently unmanaged, so an active Microsoft seller is often simply not available on those deals, regardless of how strong the partner is.

## What can an SMB partner still do when the customer is unmanaged?

Several motions survive, and this is the practical answer for a partner working below the managed-account line. A deal on an unmanaged account can still be created as a **partner-led** deal, giving Microsoft sellers visibility into your pipeline without requiring their involvement — and partner-led deals remain eligible for deal registration if the other criteria are met. What an unmanaged account rules out specifically is **Azure IP co-sell deal registration**, which requires the customer account to be Microsoft-managed. **SMB Opportunities** leads flow the other way regardless: Microsoft sellers gather requirements from a customer and hand the opportunity to a partner to run, without staying engaged. And the marketplace and lead-generation motions — profile leads, offer leads, marketplace transactions — do not depend on account coverage at all.

## Which of my own people does Microsoft's field need to know about?

Co-sell configuration requires you to nominate contacts by role and by geography, and Microsoft states this contact information is available to all Microsoft sales teams — and to channel resellers too if you make the solution available in the CSP program. Four roles are defined. **Partner marketing** markets your solution and is the main point of contact for marketing engagements and listing content. **Partner sales** sells your solution and collaborates with Microsoft sellers and channel resellers; Microsoft says include at least one per region where you want the solution co-sell-ready, and one contact can cover multiple regions. **Partner technical sales** supports technical architecture and deployment during the sales cycle and through post-sales integration. **Partner customer success manager** supports customers post-deployment to increase usage. Microsoft's emphasis: keeping these current is critical.

## What role does my company play on a services co-sell deal?

Services co-sell asks you to declare a **Partner role**, which is what the Microsoft seller uses to understand what you will actually do in the deal. The eight documented options are **Presales envisioning**, **Solution design**, **Proof of concept**, **Business strategy**, **Deployment services**, **Adoption and change management**, **Transaction**, and **Managed services**. Microsoft states that a services partner's role is assigned "based on their endorsements" — meaning the Solutions Partner designations, Specializations or Azure Expert MSP status the company has attained. Microsoft sellers evaluate services partners for co-selling using the business profile published in Partner Center, so the profile is the artefact the Microsoft field actually reads when deciding who to bring into a deal.

## How do Microsoft and a partner describe the same customer need?

Through **Solution Areas** and **Solution Plays**, the shared taxonomy Partner Center now makes mandatory on new IP co-sell and Services co-sell deals. Microsoft's stated purpose is that these fields help "Microsoft Sellers identify the customer's objectives in standard terminology" and let the seller "understand the customer needs in familiar terminology to make the partner and seller interactions seamless." The documented FY26 Solution Areas are **AI Business Solutions**, **Cloud and AI Platforms**, **Microsoft Unified** and **Security**, each with a defined set of plays such as *Copilot and Agents at Work*, *Migrate and Modernize Your Estate*, *Modern SecOps with Unified Platform* and *Secure AI Productivity*. Using the taxonomy correctly is what routes an opportunity to the right seller quickly.

## How does Microsoft expect a partner to describe deal progress?

Through MCEM stages, which Partner Center maps its own sales stages onto. Microsoft publishes the mapping: **Created**, **Accepted** and **Qualified** sit in *Listen and Consult*, exiting on a qualified opportunity; **Developed** sits in *Inspire and Design*, exiting when the customer is aligned to the solution and business case; **Proposed** and **Negotiated** sit in *Empower and Achieve*, exiting when a customer agreement is in place; and **Won** sits in *Realize Value*, where the customer has executed the agreement and deployment can begin. Marking stages is optional in general but required for solution assessment deals, and the **Won** button only enables once all prior stages are marked complete. Microsoft's stated benefit for keeping stages current is getting "stage-appropriate help from Microsoft sales representatives in a co-sell deal."

## Who do I go to for technical help from Microsoft during a sale?

A **Partner Technical Consultant**, reached through the Technical Presales and Deployment (TPD) benefit rather than through a field seller. TPD provides personalised one-to-one consultations to help plan, build and publish a Microsoft AI or cloud service and to help close sales opportunities. Eligibility and advisory hours are tiered: a **Solutions Partner designation** carries 50 advisory hours with unlimited technical presales; **Partner Success Expanded Benefits** carries 10 hours with unlimited technical presales; **Partner Success Core Benefits** carries 5 hours where technical presales deducts advisory hours; and **Partner Launch Benefits** carries neither. Microsoft states TPD is not provided to partners or resellers without one of these — including where they are on the same call as an eligible partner. After you submit a request, a Partner Technical Consultant should reach out within **two business days**.

## What will a Partner Technical Consultant help with — and what won't they?

TPD's scope is explicitly bounded, and knowing the boundary avoids wasted requests. In scope: overviews and value propositions of Microsoft products and scenarios, architecture design guidance and reviews, best practices and patterns, adapting solutions to industry scenarios, help listing in Microsoft Marketplace, **guidance on submissions to co-sell**, demo and proof-of-concept resources, technical licensing Q&A, handling customer objections, and implementation and security-posture guidance. Out of scope: training delivery, long-term consulting over 25 hours per engagement, building complete architectures, code reviews and performance tuning, application development beyond light proofs of concept, hands-on support in production, integration of non-Microsoft solutions, sales pipeline planning, end-customer discussions, licensing price lists, RFP responses, and break-fix support.

## What are the "partner roles" Microsoft uses in incentives?

A separate and narrower taxonomy from the co-sell partner roles, used in Microsoft Commerce Incentives and in the Earnings workspace filters. Microsoft defines three: **Build intent** — partner activities, advisor, and build-with — which "encourage customers to pursue Microsoft solutions and prove Microsoft solution value"; **Transact**, which "facilitate customer purchasing motions"; and **Use or consume**, which "integrate solutions into customer environments and increase consumption." These are used to filter earnings and to describe what kind of engagement generated a payment, and they do not map one-to-one onto the eight services co-sell partner roles. When a partner says "our role in that deal," it is worth establishing which taxonomy they mean.

## Can a Microsoft incentive claim turn into a co-sell deal?

Yes, and Microsoft has been building this bridge deliberately. In the MCI program, some partner activity-type engagements have referral creation and co-selling enabled. When a partner creates a claim — adding or claiming a customer and seeking their consent — the system checks the engagement and customer details, and where conditions are met it **automatically creates a partner-led referral** corresponding to the claim. The link to that referral appears on the claim detail page and partners with the Referrals admin role can open it. Once the claim receives customer consent, the partner-led referral "might get upgraded to a co-sell referral if customer qualifies for the upgrade," at which point Microsoft field sellers get visibility of the claim and can co-sell with the partner. Microsoft states plainly that it is "working to enhance MCI to support relationships with partners and help guide Microsoft's ability to co-sell with partners."

## What is not documented publicly, and what should a partner do about it

Beyond the PDM role, several things partners commonly ask about have no public Microsoft Learn source: Microsoft's segment coverage model and where the SMB / SMC-Corporate / Enterprise boundaries sit commercially; how a company becomes a "managed partner"; which Microsoft seller roles exist by title in a given geography; and how Microsoft internally decides which partner to route an SMB Opportunity to. Microsoft's own answer to questions in this space is to use the **Microsoft Partner Community** on Tech Community and **Partner Center Support**, both of which are linked from every Partner Center documentation page. For anything involving the partner's own account team or coverage, the accurate response is to direct them to those channels rather than to infer a model.

## Currency warning

The verifiable numbers on this page are program terms that change: the **14-day** Microsoft seller decision window and the **14-day** partner response window on inbound opportunities, the **two business day** TPD callback commitment, the TPD advisory hour allocations (**50 / 10 / 5 / none**) tied to specific benefit packages, and the **25-hour** per-engagement consulting cap. Solution Areas and Solution Plays are labelled **FY26** and reset with Microsoft's fiscal year. Benefit package names — Solutions Partner designation, Partner Success Expanded Benefits, Partner Success Core Benefits, Partner Launch Benefits — have themselves been renamed within the last two program years. This reflects Microsoft Learn as retrieved on 14 August 2026. Most importantly: the sections marked as unverified above should stay unverified in front of a partner. Do not supply a PDM coverage rule, a managed-partner threshold, or a segment boundary that Microsoft has not published.
