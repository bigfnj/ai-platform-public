# Packaging, pricing models and practice economics

> Source: https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/download/details.aspx?id=53132 (Azure Managed Services Playbook, © 2016) · https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/04/21/accelerating-frontier-transformation-with-microsoft-partners/ · https://partner.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/article/copilot-monthly-series-2026-issue-5
> As of: 2026-08 · Retrieved: 2026-08-14

## How do managed service providers price their offerings?

Microsoft's Azure Managed Services Playbook sets out five pricing models, and they remain a
sound taxonomy even though the document is old. **À la carte** gives each function — support,
backup, monitoring — its own SKU and meter, and Microsoft notes it is "most common for SMB
focused MSPs." **Per VM or node** charges by instance and is described as the "most common
pricing model, especially for infrastructure services." **Percentage of cloud spend** charges a
share of underlying consumption and was the "faster growing price model; typically used by
born-in-the-cloud MSPs." **Per user or device** suits managed apps and mobility offerings. **Per
project or app** covers finished solutions abstracted from infrastructure and "typically has the
highest margin," usually involving some intellectual property development.

For an SMB practice the practical read is that à-la-carte and per-user are where you start, and
per-project is where margin actually lives — which is the same conclusion Microsoft reaches from
the other direction when it talks about packaged offers today.

## How should a managed service be packaged?

As a small number of tiers, not a menu. Microsoft's playbook describes offerings "typically
priced as three tiers" and gives an **Essentials / Advanced / Premium** example that varies the
things a customer can actually feel: response time (24 hours, 1 hour, 15 minutes), backup
retention (30 days versus unlimited), and security depth (basic anti-virus versus deep security).

It also observes that "mature MSPs typically offer **3–5 distinct functions or services**." The
discipline being described is restraint. A practice with a dozen bespoke offerings has no
practice; it has a backlog.

Microsoft's current language says the same thing in modern terms: partners should build
"managed services offerings and solutions **packaged into repeatable motions**, tailored to this
customer segment," and make capability real "through packaged offers, change management and
managed operations."

## What does it cost to stand up a managed services practice?

Microsoft published figures, but they are **from 2016 and Azure-specific**, so treat them as
shape rather than budget. The playbook's own summary is a **total investment of USD 1.1–1.5
million**, comprising training at USD 800–1,000 per year, marketing at USD 8,000–12,000 per
year, software tooling at USD 50,000–60,000 per year, and **6–10 staff**. The timeline given is
**7 months to launch, 13 months to break even, 20 months to profitability**, sourced to a
"Microsoft survey of Cloud MSPs, N=50."

Microsoft's own caveat is attached and should travel with the numbers: these "represent the cost
to set up a minimally-viable MSP practice that can support 5–10 mid-sized production customers at
launch," are "representative for an organization with US based employees," and are "for
illustrative and educational purposes only."

A decade of cloud tooling maturity has almost certainly moved these figures. Use them to make the
point that a practice is a multi-quarter investment with a real J-curve, not to build a business
case.

## Why does recurring revenue matter more than the licence margin?

Because Microsoft's own commissioned research says most partner revenue no longer comes from the
licence. Omdia research sponsored by Microsoft found that "**60% of CSP partner revenue is now
tied to value-added services**, with licensing acting as the entry point to broader, services-led
engagements."

That is the single most useful figure for a recurring-revenue thesis — cite it as *Omdia
commissioned research sponsored by Microsoft, December 2025, as cited by Microsoft*, not as a
Microsoft statistic. Note also that Microsoft's own page carries transposed footnote markers
around this claim and a related marketplace figure, so quote the 60% carefully and do not attach
it to the marketplace numbers.

The playbook made the same argument in 2016 in plainer language: cloud managed services offer
"an opportunity to add a new, higher margin business line that can provide a more stable, steady
stream of recurring revenue."

## Where does AI fit into a managed services practice?

At the end of the motion, as the thing that makes it durable. Microsoft's SMB Copilot series
describes the path as starting with Copilot Chat to build familiarity, using repeatable plays to
create pipeline, converting renewal moments into Copilot Business conversations, and then
"extend[ing] value with **agents and managed services**." The final stage "is about turning that
value into a **recurring rhythm** of measurement, optimization, governance, and expansion."

Microsoft states the commercial consequence directly: "Transforming your customer with AI is not
just selling licenses. It is packaging the services, governance, analytics, skilling, and agent
lifecycle motions that make AI value measurable." A partner selling Copilot seats without a
managed motion around them has sold a licence, not built an annuity.
