# Floriography & the History of Flower Meaning

*Floriography* is the language of flowers — the practice of assigning coded
meanings to blooms so that a bouquet becomes a message. This document is the
historical backbone behind every "meaning" in the profiles.

## Ancient roots
- **Egypt, Greece, Rome:** Flowers were woven into mythology and ritual. Roses
  and myrtle tied to Aphrodite/Venus (love); the lily to Hera/Juno; narcissus
  to the myth of Narcissus; hyacinth to the youth Hyacinthus; the laurel wreath
  to victory and Apollo. Romans strewed roses at feasts (*sub rosa*, "under the
  rose," meant secrecy).
- **Persia & the Ottoman world:** A rich tradition of flower symbolism. The
  Ottoman practice of *Selam* sent messages through arrangements of objects and
  flowers — a documented ancestor of Western floriography.
- **East Asia:** China cultivated chrysanthemum and peony for millennia, each
  loaded with meaning (peony = riches and honor; chrysanthemum = longevity and
  the noble scholar). These meanings traveled to Japan and Korea.

## The Victorian language of flowers (19th century)
The best-known chapter. In 19th-century Europe and especially Britain, flower
dictionaries codified a meaning for nearly every bloom, and courtship messages
were exchanged in **tussie-mussies** (small posies).
- Popularized by books such as Charlotte de la Tour's *Le Langage des Fleurs*
  (1819) and, later, Kate Greenaway's illustrated *Language of Flowers* (1884).
- Meaning depended not just on the flower but on **presentation**: given
  upright vs. upside-down (the opposite meaning), with the right hand ("yes")
  vs. the left ("no"), and which flowers were combined.
- This era is why yellow roses can still read as "jealousy," why a striped
  carnation means "refusal," and why so many profiles carry a second, older
  meaning alongside the modern one.

## Hanakotoba (Japanese flower language)
Japan's own system, overlapping with but distinct from the Victorian code.
Examples: red rose = love; red spider lily (higanbana) = death/farewell and
final goodbyes; white chrysanthemum = truth and grief; camellia (tsubaki) =
admiration/perfect love (but the way its whole flower head drops was read by
samurai as an ill omen). Hanakotoba explains many East Asian meanings that
contradict Western ones.

## How meanings drift and conflict
- **Culture:** the same flower means opposite things across regions (see
  color-symbolism.md). Chrysanthemum = cheer in the US, death in France/Japan.
- **Color:** a single species carries a spectrum of meanings by color — always
  resolve color before stating meaning.
- **Era:** modern florist marketing has softened or overwritten older Victorian
  meanings; profiles keep both when they diverge.
- **Number & context:** a single flower vs. a dozen, a gift vs. a grave, a
  wedding vs. a funeral — context can flip the reading entirely.

## Practical takeaway for analysis
When stating what a flower "means," always: (1) fix the color, (2) name the
cultural frame, (3) give the modern reading, and (4) note the notable older or
conflicting meaning if one exists. This keeps the knowledge honest instead of
reducing a rich, contradictory tradition to a single line.
